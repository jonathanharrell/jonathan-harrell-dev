> One of the principles of durable typography is always legibility; another is something more than legibility: some earned or unearned interest that gives its living energy to the page. It takes various forms and goes by various names, including serenity, liveliness, laughter, grace and joy.
> <cite>Robert Bringhurst</cite>

Welcome to this quick cheat sheet that will you help you know which spaces, punctuation and characters to use to make your web typography great! Much of this content has been adapted from Richard Rutter's excellent handbook [Web Typography](https://book.webtypography.net/).

## Use the right spaces

### Non-breaking space

![The text “Page 2” using a non-breaking space between the word and the number. The space is emphasized with a tall red rectangle and spacing marker.](/assets/illustrations/nbsp.svg)

Use a non-breaking space to keep words together on the same line. This prevents awkward line breaks between closely related elements, such as labels and numbers.

```html
Page&nbsp;2.
```

### Thin space

![The word “you” followed by a closing single and double quotation mark, with a thin space placed between them, highlighted in red.](/assets/illustrations/thin.svg)

Use a thin space to separate characters when a regular space is too big. This maintains typographic rhythm by creating subtle breathing room but avoiding excessive spacing.

```html
Looking up he said, “She mouthed ‘I love you’&thinsp;” and then returned to his book.
```

### Hair space

![The initials “D. H. L.” spaced with thin hair spaces between each letter and period. Each space is marked with a dark vertical bar and a red spacing indicator.](/assets/illustrations/hair.svg)

Use a hair space to prevent adjacent characters touching. This adds just enough separation to avoid visual collisions between initials without disrupting flow.

```html
D.&hairsp;H.&hairsp;Lawrence.
```

### Narrow no-break space

![The expression “1⁄2 em” with a narrow no-break space between the number and the unit, highlighted by a vertical block and measurement bar beneath it.](/assets/illustrations/narrow-no-break.svg)

Use a narrow no-break space to prevent initials or numbers and their units from wrapping across two lines.

```html
A hair space is 1/24&#8239;em wide.
```

## Use the right punctuation marks

### Hyphen

![The word “op–ed” shown with a hyphen between “op” and “ed” to illustrate compound word formation.](/assets/illustrations/hyphen.svg)

Use a hyphen for one of the following:
* joining words to indicate they have a combined meaning
* indicating missing words shared by a series of compounds
* indicating stuttering speech
* splitting words when breaking them across lines

*Example:* She wore a well-tailored jacket.

### Figure dash

![The numbers 55 and 12 separated by a figure dash, illustrating the precise punctuation used for numeric identifiers.](/assets/illustrations/figure-dash.svg)

A figure dash has the same width as a numerical digit and is used within numbers to maintain alignment.

*Example:* (800‒555‒1212)

### En dash

![The numbers 4 and 5 separated by an en dash, demonstrating correct punctuation for numeric ranges.](/assets/illustrations/en-dash.svg)

An en dash indicates range with spacing that visually balances the digits it connects.

*Example:* 4–5 minutes.

### Em dash

![The phrase “I—no” with an em dash between words, highlighting punctuation for interruptions or emphasis.](/assets/illustrations/em-dash.svg)

Use an em dash to set off phrases. Separate em dashes from phrases with hair spaces. You can also use to indicate attribution after a quote, followed by a full space.

*Example:* this and that — that and this.

### Minus symbol

![Two horizontal dashes side by side: a shorter hyphen labeled with a red “X,” and a longer minus sign labeled with a green checkmark.](/assets/illustrations/minus.svg)

The minus symbol is longer and sits at the mathematical midpoint—unlike the shorter hyphen.

### Multiplication symbol

![The lowercase letter “x” on the left labeled as incorrect, contrasted with a typographic multiplication symbol on the right marked correct.](/assets/illustrations/multiplication.svg)

A proper multiplication sign avoids confusion with the letter “x” and aligns better with numerals.

### Obelus

![A slash symbol on the left marked incorrect and labeled “slash,” contrasted with a division sign (obelus) on the right marked correct.](/assets/illustrations/obelus.svg)

The obelus is the correct symbol for division, with visual weight that centers between operands.

### Quotation marks & apostrophes

![Four straight vertical quote marks on the left labeled “straight marks” and marked with a red X. On the right, matching typographic curly quotes marked correct.](/assets/illustrations/quotes.svg)

![On the left: two straight vertical marks labeled “straight marks” and marked incorrect. On the right: typographic apostrophes with curled shapes marked correct.](/assets/illustrations/apostrophes.svg)

Use proper quotation marks and apostrophes (not straight marks).

### Ellipsis

![Two sets of ellipses shown side by side: three individual dots on the left marked incorrect, and a single ellipsis character on the right marked correct.](/assets/illustrations/ellipsis.svg)

The proper ellipsis character (instead of three individual dots) ensures consistent spacing and baseline alignment across typefaces.

### Primes

![On the left, three straight vertical tick marks labeled “straight marks” and marked incorrect; on the right, slanted tick marks representing prime and double prime symbols are marked correct.](/assets/illustrations/primes.svg)

Prime marks indicate units like inches and minutes; straight apostrophes are typographic imposters.

### Degree symbol

![A lowercase letter “o” on the left marked incorrect, contrasted with a correctly raised and smaller degree symbol on the right.](/assets/illustrations/degree.svg)

The proper degree symbol is smaller and raised—distinct from a lowercase o.

### Parentheses

![Two sets of parentheses compared side by side. On the left, the italicized version is marked incorrect; on the right, the roman version is marked correct, showing the preferred typographic form.](/assets/illustrations/parentheses.svg)

Parentheses and brackets are not designed to be italicized; they retain proper shape and spacing when set in roman.
